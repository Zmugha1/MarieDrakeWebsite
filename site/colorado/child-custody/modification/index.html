Colorado Child Custody Modification

A parenting order that made sense at the time of your original case may not fit your life today. When circumstances change, Colorado courts allow either parent to request a modification to an existing divorce-related parenting time and parenting responsibilities order or an allocation of parental responsibilities (APR) order where parties were never married. But the process has a real legal threshold, a defined procedure, and a timeline that rewards acting sooner rather than later.

What Custody Modification Means in Colorado

Colorado statutes do not use the word “custody.” What most people mean by custody is formally called an order regarding parenting responsibilities and parenting time in a divorce or an order regarding allocation of parental responsibilities (APR) if parties were never married. Both modifications fall under C.R.S. 14-10-129
[1]. Parenting time and parenting responsibilities have two components: decision-making responsibility (who makes major calls about education, healthcare, and religion) and parenting time (the schedule for when the child is with each parent). A modification can target either component, both, or emergency provisions, depending on what has changed.

When you say you want to “change custody,” you likely mean more parenting time, a change in decision-making authority, or both. The legal standard that applies depends on the scope of what you are asking the court to do.

The Legal Standard: Two Tiers Depending on What You Are Asking For

Colorado does not apply a single modification standard to every request. The applicable threshold depends on the scope of the change you are seeking.

Tier 1: Best interests only. If you are seeking a change to parenting time that does not alter the majority residential parent, the court applies the best-interests standard under C.R.S. 14-10-129(1)(a)(I) [1]. The court may modify parenting time whenever doing so would serve the child’s best interests.

Tier 2: Substantial change plus endangerment. If you are seeking a modification that substantially changes parenting time and changes which parent the child lives with the majority of the time, the standard is higher. Under C.R.S. 14-10-129(2)
[1], the court will not grant that modification unless it finds, based on facts that arose after or were unknown at the time of the prior order, that a change has occurred in the circumstances of the child or the majority-time parent, and that modification is necessary to serve the child’s best interests.

Within that higher tier, the endangerment exception applies: under C.R.S. 14-10-129(2)(d)
[1], a court may modify a prior order when the child’s present environment endangers the child’s physical health or significantly impairs the child’s emotional development, and the harm from continuing the arrangement is outweighed by the advantage of changing it.

In plain terms: the more significant the change you are seeking, the harder the court looks. Knowing which tier applies to your situation is the first strategic question.

What Courts Recognize as a Changed Circumstance

Before a court will consider a Tier 2 modification, you must establish that circumstances have changed since the prior order. Courts that have addressed this include circumstances such as:

  • Relocation by either parent, particularly when it significantly impacts the child’s access to the other parent
  • A child’s changed developmental needs, such as new special education requirements, a significant medical diagnosis, or a major schooling change
  • A parent’s major life change, including remarriage, job loss, incarceration, or a serious health issue affecting parenting capacity
  • Evidence of endangerment or abuse, including domestic violence, substance abuse, or documented neglect
  • Consistent parenting-time violations, where one parent is repeatedly interfering with the other’s court-ordered time
  • A child’s stated preferences, depending on the child’s age and maturity (courts weigh this, but it is not determinative on its own)

What does not clear the threshold: normal life transitions, general frustration with the schedule, or short-term disruptions that are not ongoing. Courts are designed to discourage repeated filings over temporary friction. The change must be real, material, and continuing.

When Your Child's Safety Is the Issue

The endangerment standard under C.R.S. 14-10-129(2)(d) requires a three-part showing: first, that the prior order shall be presumed to stand; second, that the child’s present environment endangers the child’s physical health or significantly impairs emotional development, and that the harm from a change is outweighed by the advantage of changing; third, that the proposed modification is in the child’s best interests.

This standard is demanding even though it allows modification without showing a broader change in circumstances. Accusations alone do not carry cases. Police reports, medical records, school records, therapist notes, and witness statements all build the evidentiary record courts actually examine.

If your child is in immediate danger right now, a standard modification petition is not the right tool. Emergency motions to restrict parenting time are heard within 14 days of filing. Call us before you make any move.

The Colorado Modification Process, Step by Step

Modification cases are filed in the same Colorado district court that entered the original parenting order. Jefferson County cases are handled at the Jefferson County Combined Court (1st Judicial District). Here is what the process looks like:

  1. File a Motion to Modify Parental Responsibilities and Parenting Time. You or your attorney file in the originating district court. The motion must identify the specific relief requested and the grounds for the modification. This document sets the framework for everything that follows.
  2. Serve the other parent. Proper service is required under the Colorado Rules of Civil Procedure. The other parent has the opportunity to respond, object, or file a counter-motion. The response deadline runs from the date of service.
  3. Initial case management and mediation. Courts frequently require mediation before setting a hearing. Colorado policy favors parents resolving post-decree disputes without judicial intervention when possible. In many cases, mediation is not optional.
  4. Evaluation, if ordered. In contested cases, the court may appoint a Child and Family Investigator (CFI) or Parental Responsibilities Evaluator (PRE) to assess the situation and make recommendations. This step adds meaningful time and cost.
  5. Evidentiary hearing. If no agreement is reached, the case proceeds to hearing where both parents present evidence and testimony. The judge applies the best-interests factors under C.R.S. 14-10-124 [2].
  6. Court order. The judge enters a written Order modifying the current Parenting Responsibilities and Parenting Time Order if it is post-divorce or a written Order Modifying modified the current APR order if parties were never married. That new order controls until a future modification is sought and granted.

Timeline matters. Uncontested modifications can resolve in a few months once paperwork is processed. Contested modifications involving a CFI or PRE commonly take six months to over a year. Do not underestimate the timeline when planning school enrollment, childcare, or relocation decisions.

What Colorado Courts Look At: The Best-Interests Factors

Once the court clears the applicable threshold, the analysis shifts to whether the proposed modification serves the child’s best interests. C.R.S. 14-10-124
[2] sets out the factors the court must consider. The full list includes:

  • The wishes of each parent as to parenting time
  • The wishes of the child, if the child is mature enough to express reasoned and independent preferences
  • The child’s relationship with each parent, siblings, and others who significantly affect the child’s interests
  • The child’s adjustment to home, school, and community
  • The mental and physical health of all individuals involved (disability alone is not a basis to restrict parenting time)
  • Each parent’s ability to encourage sharing of love, affection, and contact with the other parent (protecting a child from witnessing abuse is not held against the protective parent)
  • Whether the past pattern of involvement of each parent reflects a system of values, time commitment, and mutual support
  • The physical proximity of the parents as it relates to the practical logistics of parenting time
  • The ability of each parent to place the child’s needs ahead of their own

This list is not exhaustive. Courts consider all relevant factors. The weight given to each factor depends on the specific facts of your case.

Colorado courts do not disrupt stable arrangements without compelling reason. If your child is adjusted and thriving, the bar for modification is higher. That is not a reason to give up. It is a signal to build a stronger record. We help you do that.

Your Parenting Plan Will Need to Be Updated

Any modification to parenting time or decision-making requires an updated Colorado parenting plan that reflects the new arrangement in writing. A well-drafted parenting plan specifies the schedule, holidays, school breaks, communication rules, and how future disagreements get resolved. It is not a formality. It is the document you and your co-parent will live by, and getting it right the first time prevents the disputes that bring people back to court.

When Parents Agree vs. When They Do Not

If both parents agree to the modification, the process is far simpler. You submit a written stipulation to the court, the judge reviews it against the best-interests standard, and an order enters. You bypass the evidentiary hearing entirely.

One thing cannot be shortcut: the stipulation must be properly drafted. An informal text-message agreement is not legally enforceable. It creates problems later.

When the other parent contests the modification, the process is more involved, more expensive, and harder on everyone, including your child. That is not a reason to back down if the change is warranted. It means you need a strategy before you file: what evidence to gather, whether mediation is worth pursuing first, whether a CFI appointment helps or hurts your position, and what the realistic range of outcomes looks like at hearing.

We prefer resolving cases without trial. If the other parent is cooperative, we push hard for agreement. If they are not, if they are violating the current order, blocking your access, or creating real risk for your child, we will not hesitate to litigate. No Drama Divorce does not mean no spine.

Custody Changes Often Affect Child Support

Significant shifts in parenting time, particularly changes in overnight stays, typically affect the child support calculation under Colorado’s income shares guidelines. If your modification results in a substantially different time allocation, a child support modification should be addressed in the same proceeding or immediately after. Addressing both together is more efficient and prevents stale support orders that no longer reflect the actual arrangement.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a custody modification take in Colorado?

Uncontested modifications can resolve in a few months once the court processes the stipulation. Contested cases involving a CFI evaluation or evidentiary hearing typically take six months to over a year. The timeline depends on the court’s docket, whether the parties can agree, and whether an evaluator is appointed.

Can I modify custody without going to court?

If both parents agree, you can submit a written stipulation for court approval without a full hearing. You still need a signed court order. A verbal agreement or a text exchange is not legally enforceable and sets up problems down the road. The process is much simpler with agreement, but the court must still enter the modified order.

What if my child is in danger right now?

Do not file a standard modification petition. Colorado courts can enter emergency orders restricting or suspending parenting time when there is credible, documented evidence of immediate risk. Emergency motions under C.R.S. 14-10-129(4) are heard within 14 days. Call us immediately.

How often can custody be modified in Colorado?

There is no statutory cap on the number of modifications. But courts are not interested in repeated filings based on the same facts. Each new motion petition requires a new and continuing substantial change. Filing too soon after a prior modification, without genuinely new grounds, risks dismissal and can affect your standing with the court going forward.

Do I need an attorney for a custody modification?

You are not legally required to have one. But the two-tier standard, the evidentiary requirements, the procedural rules, and the nuances of what actually moves Jefferson County judges are not things you want to figure out as you go. A wrong move on the threshold analysis or a poorly drafted stipulation is harder and more expensive to fix than getting it right the first time. We give you a straight read from the first call, whether or not that leads to hiring us.

Unfit Parents

Sadly, some parents are unfit to raise children. If a parent is an alcoholic, abusive, neglectful, or mentally ill, he or she might be deemed unfit. Then, the other parent can receive sole physical and legal custody. If you think the other parent is unfit, speak to your  child custody lawyer. Your child’s safety and well-being are at risk, so you need to move forward and take action quickly.

Unmarried Fathers

In Colorado, if a child is born to parents who are not married, the mother is automatically granted both full legal and physical custody of the child. The father does not have any custody or visitation rights unless a court order is obtained or if the parents reach an agreement. It’s important to note that the birth of a child does not immediately establish a legal parent-child relationship in this situation.

Can The Child Decide Where To Live?

While the child cannot choose the custody arrangement, the court will consider his or her opinion. First, the judge will assess the child’s maturity. If the judge believes he or she is mature enough to understand the situation, the child can then provide an opinion. The child’s opinion is just one of many factors that go into developing custody agreements.
